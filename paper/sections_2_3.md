## 2. Related work

**Sky-image deep learning for solar forecasting.** Sky cameras observe the advecting cloud field directly and have become the dominant sensor for intra-hour forecasting. Sun et al. (2019) introduced SUNSET, a convolutional network ingesting a hybrid of sky images and PV history for 15-minute-ahead minutely PV power, establishing that imagery and recent power are jointly informative and that training on power (rather than clear-sky index) is preferable. Paletta et al. (2021) benchmarked CNN and ConvLSTM forecasters on sky images and exposed a recurring pathology: many deep models barely exceed smart persistence at short horizons because they learn to mimic persistence with a temporal lag, underscoring the necessity of honest skill reporting against persistence. Paletta et al. (2022) advanced spatio-temporal modelling with ECLIPSE, predicting future sky images and irradiance to anticipate cloud-induced ramps. The community's datasets and methods were surveyed by Nie et al. (2024b), who identified standardized probabilistic evaluation as an open need.

**The SKIPP'D benchmark and generative probabilistic forecasting.** Nie et al. (2023) released SKIPP'D, three years of quality-controlled 64×64 sky images and concurrent 1-minute PV power for a 30-kW rooftop array, now the standard benchmark for image-based PV forecasting. On it, Nie et al. (2024a) proposed SkyGPT: a physics-constrained VideoGPT generates ten stochastic future sky-image scenarios that a U-Net maps to a probabilistic 15-minute PV forecast. SkyGPT is the state of the art in sharpness on SKIPP'D, reporting CRPS 2.81 kW and a Winkler score of 26.70 against SUNSET (3.31; 56.95) and smart persistence (3.67). Two properties of this line of work define the gap we address. First, it is reported at a **single 15-minute horizon**; reserve scheduling, ramp response, and unit commitment occur at different lead times. Second, it reports **sharpness only** — CRPS and Winkler score — with no PICP, empirical coverage, reliability diagram, or PIT, so whether its intervals are calibrated is unestablished.

**Verification of probabilistic solar forecasts.** The verification literature is explicit that a probabilistic forecast must be assessed on *both* calibration (reliability) and sharpness (Lauret et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020). Calibration is the property an operator depends on: a 90% interval must contain the truth 90% of the time. Conformal prediction supplies this in a distribution-free manner, and recent PV studies show post-hoc conformal calibration brings empirical coverage to ≈90.8–91.4% at a 90% nominal target while controlling interval width. No sky-image forecaster has yet demonstrated verified coverage; SolarSDE does.

**Neural differential equations and latent stochastic dynamics.** Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018) parameterize continuous-time latent dynamics; latent neural SDEs (Li et al., 2020; Kidger et al., 2021) extend this to a distribution over trajectories, giving native uncertainty but requiring simulation-based training and lacking closed-form likelihoods. We specialize this framework to a mixture of Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes whose Gaussian marginals are analytic at every horizon, removing the SDE solver at inference and enabling a differentiable closed-form CRPS objective.

**Probabilistic UQ baselines and economic value.** MC-Dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016) and conditional score-based diffusion (CSDI; Tashiro et al., 2021) are standard probabilistic baselines, which we include. On the value side, Wang et al. (2022) show probabilistic solar forecasts informing CAISO flexible-ramp procurement cut uncertainty-related costs by roughly 90% over a representative period, motivating our reserve-value study.

## 3. Method

SolarSDE comprises (i) a cloud-state encoder producing a latent trajectory and a Cloud Turbulence Index, (ii) a CTI-gated mixture-of-Ornstein–Uhlenbeck latent SDE with closed-form marginals and a persistence anchor, and (iii) group-conditional conformal calibration. We summarize each component; full derivations are in Appendix A.

### 3.1 Cloud-state encoding and the Cloud Turbulence Index
A convolutional variational autoencoder encodes each 64×64 all-sky image to a 64-dimensional cloud-state vector *z*ₜ (Eq. A.1). From the latent trajectory we compute the Cloud Turbulence Index, the ℓ₂-norm of the per-dimension variance of the latent velocity over a 10-frame window (Eq. A.2). CTI is large at cloud edges and broken-cloud conditions and near zero under uniform clear or overcast sky; it is robustly normalized to *O*(1) dynamic range so it can modulate the diffusion.

### 3.2 CTI-gated mixture-of-OU latent SDE
We forecast the clear-sky-index residual *δkₜ₊ₕ* = *kₜ₊ₕ* − *kₜ* as the marginal of a mixture of *K* = 3 Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes (Eq. A.3) whose diffusion coefficient is gated by the CTI (Eq. A.4): the stochastic forcing is amplified precisely when the cloud field is turbulent — a physically-grounded, sharpness-on-demand mechanism. Because the OU process has a Gaussian marginal in closed form (Eq. A.5), the predictive law at *any* horizon is an analytic Gaussian mixture, obtained without autoregressive rollout or sky-image generation. A learnable persistence anchor (Eq. A.6) blends in a *N*(0, σ²pers(*h*)) component with a per-horizon weight cap, guaranteeing the forecast degrades to a strong baseline at the shortest horizons where the clear-sky index is near-perfectly autocorrelated.

### 3.3 Closed-form CRPS training
The model is trained by minimizing the continuous ranked probability score of the blended Gaussian mixture against the realized residual. CRPS admits an exact, differentiable closed form for Gaussian mixtures (Eq. A.7–A.8), so gradients flow through the mixture weights, OU parameters, and the persistence-blend weight without a sampling-based estimator — which is what permits stable training of the blend.

### 3.4 Group-conditional conformal calibration
After training, predictive intervals are calibrated on the validation set within each (horizon, CTI-quartile) cell — Mondrian conformal prediction (Eq. A.9). For each cell we select the multiplicative scale that minimizes validation CRPS subject to an empirical coverage floor, calibrating the reported metric directly rather than assuming Gaussianity. This yields the verified coverage reported in Section 4.5.
